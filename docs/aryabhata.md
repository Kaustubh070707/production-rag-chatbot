# Aryabhata — Ancient Indian Astronomer

Source: Wikipedia article "Aryabhata" (CC BY-SA 4.0, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aryabhata, retrieved 2026). Condensed extract for demo retrieval corpus.

Aryabhata I (476-550 CE) was the first of the major mathematician-astronomers from the classical age of Indian mathematics and Indian astronomy. His works include the Aryabhatiya (in 499 CE, when he was 23 years old) and the Arya-siddhanta.

Aryabhata called himself a native of Kusumapura or Pataliputra (near present-day Patna, Bihar). He went to Kusumapura for advanced studies and lived there. He was the head of an institution (kulapa) at Kusumapura, and he is reputed to have set up an observatory at the Sun temple in Taregana, Bihar.

The Aryabhatiya has 108 verses plus 13 introductory verses, divided into Gitikapada, Ganitapada, Kalakriyapada and Golapada. The mathematical part covers arithmetic, algebra, plane and spherical trigonometry, continued fractions, quadratic equations and sums-of-power series, with a table of sines.

Aryabhata worked on the approximation for pi. He gives circumference 62,832 for diameter 20,000, which is 3.1416, accurate to two parts in a million. He may have meant the value is incommensurable.

Aryabhata discussed sine by the name ardha-jya, shortened to jya. Arabic writers rendered it jiba, later misunderstood as jaib, and Gerard of Cremona translated it to Latin sinus — hence sine. He defined sine (jya), cosine (kojya), versine and inverse sine, and gave sine and versine tables in 3.75 degree intervals to 4 decimal places.

Aryabhata stated the Earth rotates on its axis daily and that the apparent motion of stars is relative motion caused by Earth's rotation. He described a geocentric model with epicycles, explained eclipses as shadows cast by and falling on Earth, and calculated sidereal rotation as 23 hours 56 minutes 4.1 seconds.

His astronomical calculation methods were widely used in the Islamic world for Arabic astronomical tables (zijes). The tables of Al-Zarqali (11th century), translated as the Tables of Toledo (12th century), remained Europe's most accurate ephemeris for centuries. His calendar calculations fed the Jalali calendar introduced in 1073 CE by astronomers including Omar Khayyam, still the basis of national calendars in Iran and Afghanistan.

India's first satellite Aryabhata and the lunar crater Aryabhata are named in his honour, as is the Aryabhatta Research Institute of Observational Sciences (ARIES) near Nainital. His successor Bhaskara I wrote commentaries Laghubhaskariya and Mahabhaskariya following Aryabhata's system.
